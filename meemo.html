Button Actuation Mechanism — Meemo Wristbuddy

Role: mechanical design intern at Meemo — I designed the button mechanism and integrated the status indicator into the existing enclosure · Tools: Onshape, SOLIDWORKS, FDM printing · Timeline: Summer 2025 · Product: the Wristbuddy parent–child locating device

Section through the button stack. The whole mechanism lives between the inside face of the shell and the top of a switch.

Designed the three-button input mechanism for the Meemo Wristbuddy, a parent–child locating device — working inside an enclosure and a circuit board that were both already fixed. Each button is a capped plunger passing through the top shell, retained from below and travel-matched to the tactile switch on the board — then verified in assembly against the populated PCB model.

The constraint

The Wristbuddy is a wearable that lets a parent locate a child, so it gets used one-handed, in a hurry, often without looking at it. This was the opposite of a clean-sheet problem, and that’s what made it interesting. The enclosure existed. The board existed, with its switches already placed. The product needed three front-panel inputs and a status light, and neither the shell’s outer form nor the board layout was mine to move.

So the entire design space was the few millimetres between the inside face of the top shell and the top of a tactile switch. Everything had to be solved in that gap: how the button is held in, how far it travels, how it stays square, and how it feels.

How the mechanism works

  • Cap. Sits proud of the shell so it can be found and pressed without looking — this is a device used one-handed, often without attention.
  • Stem through the shell wall. The through-hole guides the plunger and keeps it square, so an off-centre press still actuates cleanly instead of binding.
  • Retention collar below the shell face. Larger than the through-hole, so the button cannot fall out through the front. It also sets the rest position, which is what fixes the travel.
  • Travel matched to the switch. The gap from plunger tip to switch dome sets how far the cap moves before actuation, and the collar bottoming out limits over-travel so the user can’t drive the plunger into the switch past its rated stroke.
  • Three in an array, on the same pitch as the switches on the board.

Status indicator

Alongside the buttons I integrated an addressable RGB indicator into the top shell — a single package driving all the device’s status colours from one opening rather than a separate LED per state. The work was placing the emitter and its aperture where the light reads clearly from outside without fouling anything already occupying that volume inside.

Verification

A mechanism that works in isolation isn’t worth much, so the shell went into an assembly with the fully populated board model — every connector, module and passive in place. That’s where a packaging design actually gets tested: whether the plungers land on their switches, whether anything collides through the travel, and whether the connectors are still reachable once the shell is closed.

Printed and pressed

The mechanism was 3D printed and tested by hand against the switches — the point being to check the things CAD can’t tell you: whether the plunger actuates without binding when pressed off-centre, whether the collar retains it, and whether the press feels like a button rather than like pushing on a wall. (No photos of that build survive, which is the one thing I’d do differently — photograph every prototype.)

What this is, and isn’t

This is mechanism design and packaging. I did no wiring and no firmware — the indicator is placed and integrated mechanically, not driven. Validation was functional hand-testing of a printed part, not instrumented measurement.

What I’d change

  • Run a tolerance stack. Plunger travel is the sum of shell thickness, collar position, board standoff height and switch height — four tolerances stacking into one clearance. I set it nominally and confirmed it worked on one printed part. That’s not the same as confirming it works at the tolerance extremes, where the button could fail to actuate at one end or sit pre-loaded on the switch at the other.
  • Measure the actuation force. The switch has a rated actuation force, but what the user feels also includes friction in the guide. I judged it by hand; a gauge would turn that into a number and let it be tuned deliberately.
  • Cycle it. A press-and-release test to a few thousand cycles would show whether the collar or the guide bore wears, which is the failure mode a printed part hides and a moulded one inherits.
