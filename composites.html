Composites Manufacturing — Plug to Mold to Part

Role: Mechanical Engineering Intern, Levanta Technologies — hands-on composites work alongside the engineering team · Skills: plug prep, tooling gelcoat, chopped-strand mold layup, carbon wet layup, vacuum bagging, demolding & trimming · Timeline: Jun – Aug 2026

Nine weeks of production composites at a maritime drone startup. I worked every stage of the tooling chain — surfacing plugs, laying tooling gelcoat, building chopped-strand fiberglass molds, wet-laying carbon into them, vacuum bagging, demolding and trimming — and the capstone was the vehicle’s full-length carbon bottom skin, taken from a primed plug to a demolded, hull-fitted part in about eight days. The through-line of the summer: in composites the tooling is the engineering. Every defect in the part was already present in the mold.

Two generations before the part

A composite part doesn’t start at the part. It starts at a plug — the positive shape, surfaced until it’s worth copying — and then a mold cast over it, which is the negative that every finished part will inherit. Any scratch, low spot, or missed seam on the plug gets faithfully reproduced in the mold and then reproduced again in every part that comes out of it. Most of the hours go into surfaces nobody will ever see in the final assembly.

My work on smaller foil and strut parts covered that full chain: prepping and sealing foam plugs, taping foil and film for release, fairing seams, then building female molds over them in box forms — tooling gelcoat first, then chopped-strand mat wetted out and rolled to chase air out of every corner and radius. A roller in a corner radius is not busywork; trapped air there becomes a void in the mold face, which becomes a bump on every part.

Into the mold: layup, bagging, demold, trim

With tools in hand the cycle is wet layup, bag, cure, demold, trim. Bagging a three-dimensional part is a different problem from bagging a flat panel — film has to be pleated into every concave corner or it bridges, and wherever the bag bridges the laminate underneath simply doesn’t get consolidated. Vacuum is not a magic setting you dial in; it’s a physical argument you make with the film about where it’s allowed to touch.

The capstone: the full-length carbon bottom skin

The big one ran the same chain at a scale where mistakes are expensive and nothing can be redone quickly. The plug got primed and faired, a full-length fiberglass mold was laid up over it and stiffened with wooden ribs so a tool that size wouldn’t flex, and then the whole part — carbon, wetted out, bagged, and drawn down in one go — had to be laid up inside a single working pot life.

Layup day started before sunrise, because once the resin is mixed the schedule is set by chemistry rather than by preference — every ply, every pleat, and every sealant-tape joint has to be done inside the pot life, in order, with no chance to stop and think. Demolded the next morning, trimmed, and test-fitted to the hull the same day.

What this summer actually taught me

  • Surface prep is the product. Nothing downstream fixes a bad plug — every flaw is copied into the mold and then into every part off it. The unglamorous sanding hours are the ones that determine part quality.
  • Consolidation is a physical argument, not a setting. Where the bag bridges, the laminate isn’t consolidated. Pleats, breather placement, and port position are the real controls; the gauge reading only tells you the bag isn’t leaking.
  • Working time is a schedule constraint. Pot life turns a big layup into a choreographed operation. You plan the sequence and stage every material before anything gets mixed.

All three transferred immediately: I reused the same tooling logic the same week on my own carbon nosecone at home — where I learned the corollary the hard way, that a 3D-printed mold is not a vacuum barrier, and the film has to be the barrier instead.

What I’d change

I’d cut cured-ply-thickness coupons for every new cloth and resin combination rather than carrying a manufacturer’s number into a wall-thickness calculation — the same gap I ran into later on my own parts, where cured thickness depends on consolidation pressure and therefore on the process, not on the datasheet. And I’d want to own a layup schedule end to end — ply count, orientations, and overlap strategy specified by me and then verified on the cured part — rather than executing someone else’s.
