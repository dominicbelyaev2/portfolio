Retractable Keel Fin — A Passive Mechanism With Contradictory Orders

Role: Mechanical Engineering Intern, Levanta Technologies — owned the fin mechanism end-to-end under the engineering lead’s direction · Tools: Fusion 360, ANSYS Fluent (2D + 3D CFD), CadQuery, Shigley spring design, FDM prototyping · Timeline: Jun – Aug 2026

The final twin-fin unit, and the kick-up demo: the blades fold away on contact, then spring back to deployed.

A passive, spring-loaded keel fin that has to stay rigid against a 50-knot water load — and then fold flat when the vehicle drives itself up onto a recovery platform. No electronics, no motors, no actuators. The requirement contradicts itself on its face; this project is the architecture that makes both true at once, and the analysis chain that sized it. Delivered as a demonstrated prototype and a design package.

The brief — and the contradiction inside it

The vehicle is an autonomous maritime drone that loiters at sea and later recovers itself by swimming nose-first onto a raft or deck. A keel fin at the tail gives it directional stability underway. That fin is exactly what you do not want protruding when the hull slides up onto a flat platform.

The requirements I was handed, essentially verbatim: a simple system with minimal failure points; spring-loaded and passive; separate the drag force from the lateral stability force; corrosion-resistant components; hundreds to thousands of load cycles; must not retract under normal load in the water, must retract when hitting the raft to climb on; a maximum speed of about 50 knots; and it must survive the impact of getting up onto the raft. Two constraints came with it — keep the specified NACA 16-006 section, and keep the part manufacturable in composites.

One number on this page is mine rather than the brief’s: the stated cycle requirement is “hundreds/thousands,” but a multi-week loiter at wave frequency implies something nearer a million reversals, which makes it a corrosion-fatigue problem rather than a static one. I designed against my own harsher assumption, and I label it as an assumption.

The architecture that resolves it: decouple the loads

The reason a passive spring is feasible at all is that the two loads on this fin are wildly different sizes and do not have to share a path. At yaw, the lateral force on the blade is roughly 35× the drag force. If that lateral force reaches the spring, the spring is fighting the ocean continuously and nothing closes.

So it doesn’t. The blade pivots about a spanwise axis at its root. Lateral force acts along that axis and gets reacted by the hinge pin and load-bearing knuckles as shear and bending — producing essentially no moment about the pivot. Only drag makes the fin want to fold. The spring therefore only has to beat the much smaller drag moment while the structure quietly eats the big one. The fin also sits at zero angle of incidence when deployed, which minimizes drag and maximizes directional stability at the same time.

The springs are a matched left-hand/right-hand torsion pair flanking a keyed collar, sitting at their free angle when deployed — no preload, near-zero torque at rest. Folding toward stowed winds them up through the full 80° sweep. A shaft that merely passes through a coil transmits nothing, so torque is routed deliberately: hull → fixed leg → coil → moving leg → keyed collar → shaft → fin.

Putting a number on the load — then correcting it twice

The spring rate follows from one number: the hinge moment the water applies to the deployed fin. First pass was a hand calculation — dynamic pressure in seawater at 25.72 m/s is about 339 kPa, drag is q·S·CD, and the moment is that force acting through the taper’s drag centroid. I deliberately assumed a very conservative CD = 0.10 to get a bounding value: roughly 220 N of drag and a hinge moment near 12 N·m.

Two corrections landed on that number. The first was mine to catch and embarrassing in the best way: my calculator was in radian mode, so sin 70 evaluated to 0.774 instead of 0.940. Two mistakes had been partially cancelling — I had also used half-span as the moment arm instead of the taper’s actual drag centroid at 53.5 mm. Corrected, the installed moment is about 1.26 N·m. The second correction was the CFD, which showed the “conservative” bound wasn’t conservative so much as wrong by an order of magnitude.

CFD: replacing a guess with a number, then checking the number

I built a 2D case for the section: NACA 16-006 at a 57 mm chord, seawater (ρ = 1025 kg/m³, μ = 0.00107 Pa·s) at 25.72 m/s, Re ≈ 1.4×106, k-ω SST, steady and pressure-based, domain to ±12 chords with roughly 32 inflation layers and 200 chordwise divisions. Wall resolution came out at y+ = 1.58 — under the target of 5 and near the ideal of 1, so the boundary layer is genuinely resolved rather than modeled. Result: Cd = 0.0111 at zero incidence, 214 N per meter of span, split 76% viscous / 24% pressure.

Getting there taught the setup more than the answer did. Setting the farfield to “normal to boundary” instead of explicit velocity components produced colliding jets and a confident, absurd result — Cd = 0.234 with Cl = −7.16 on a symmetric foil at zero incidence. An answer that wrong is easy to catch. The dangerous ones are the plausible ones, which is why the next step mattered.

I validated the solver against something it could not have known. Turbulent flat-plate skin friction is closed-form and carries no pressure drag, making it a pure friction benchmark: Cf = 0.074/Re0.2 gives 0.00436 per side, 0.0087 for two sides — against the CFD’s viscous Cd = 0.0086. A 2% match, where a laminar assumption would have sat four times lower. That agreement is why I trust the drag number at all.

I did have to walk back one claim about my own result. I’d argued that because XFOIL’s panel method bottoms out below my value, the flow must be fully turbulent. That’s a non-sequitur — XFOIL simply doesn’t model bypass or roughness transition. The honest position: fully-turbulent is a conservative bound, with the true Cd bracketed between roughly 0.005 and 0.0115, plus physical reasons to expect the turbulent end — the hydraulically-smooth limit is about 4 μm, a molded surface runs 5–15 μm, and biofouling over a long deployment exceeds it by orders of magnitude.

The headline: the conservative guess was about 9× too big. A measured Cd of 0.0111 against the assumed 0.10 turns 220 N of drag into roughly 25 N, and drops the hinge moment from about 12 N·m to ~1.3 N·m. That is not a rounding difference — a spring sized for 12 N·m could not have packaged into the hinge at all. Three independent routes (2D CFD, the corrected hand calc, and the first 3D run) landed in the same band, so I moved the design point to the measurement with normal margin instead of staying anchored to a bound I already knew was false.

Auditing my own results

The first 3D run produced three separate reporting bugs, each of which would have handed me a confident wrong number:

  • The drag-coefficient monitor read 5.37×10−5 — meaningless, because the reference area was left at Fluent’s 1 m² default. The proof is arithmetic: dividing the drag force by that coefficient returns exactly the freestream dynamic pressure. Corrected against real planform area, Cd = 0.0083.
  • The hinge-moment monitor read exactly zero on every iteration. Not physics — when I created the report I zeroed only the Z field of the moment axis and never set Y to 1, leaving a null axis vector. A one-field fix, but a monitor that reads a clean 0.0000 for 53 iterations is exactly the kind of output that gets quoted.
  • About 30 N of side force on a symmetric foil at zero incidence, where the answer should be zero. That’s a mesh-asymmetry flag. It doesn’t contaminate the hinge moment, but it disqualifies this mesh for the sideslip sweep — 30 N of phantom force would drown the signal I’d be looking for.

The mesh itself was a deliberate trade I wrote down before running it. Wall-resolved inflation layers on a 0.5 mm blunt trailing edge and sharp tip corners destroyed element quality, and a properly resolved 3D boundary layer blows past the 512,000-cell student solver limit before spanwise cells even exist. So I dropped inflation, kept a healthy tetrahedral mesh, and stated the cost up front: y+ in the hundreds, and the viscous share — which is 76% of total drag — under-resolved. The run came in 26% below the 2D-scaled prediction, which is exactly the direction and roughly the magnitude that trade predicts. A bounded answer from a healthy mesh beats a precise-looking answer from a broken one.

Roads not taken

Before the plain spring won, several mechanisms got sketched and killed — each on a specific reason rather than taste:

  • A flush trigger button in the airfoil’s lower surface, latching the fin rigid until the platform pressed it. Killed on failure modes: the button can miss the raft entirely, and it can jam. It did leave behind a principle I kept — decouple the water-flow force direction from the release-force direction.
  • Ratchet-and-pawl auto-relock with asymmetric sawtooth teeth. Genuinely workable, and kept as the fallback in case a plain spring couldn’t both hold at speed and release on contact. It stopped being justifiable once the real hinge moment came in 9× lower.
  • A torsion bar instead of coil springs — killed by arithmetic. A bar of the available diameter twists about 17° in the 60 mm of hinge length on offer; the mechanism needs 80°. Getting there wants a 300–440 mm bar. A coil spring is the packaging solution: roughly 600 mm of wire folded into a 45 mm body.
  • Four small springs instead of two, to shrink the housing. Halving per-spring torque thins the wire only as the cube root, while coil diameter is floored at shaft plus two wire diameters — so it saves about a millimetre of diameter and costs 25% more axial length, which was already the binding dimension.

Spring sizing — and reporting that my own prototype springs were disqualifying

Torsion-spring wire works in bending, so sizing followed Shigley’s method: stress from 32M/πd³ with the curvature correction, rate from E·d4/(10.8·D·N), allowable at 0.61·Sut for stainless. Accepting about 10° of bend-back at the operating moment fixes the total rate, which then splits across the matched pair. The binding constraint turned out to be neither stress nor rate but axial stack-up: every variant that made the required torque wanted 37–50 mm of coil body per spring, on a hinge about 60 mm long.

The catalog springs I had been demonstrating with top out near 0.85 N·m — roughly six times too weak for the stowed condition. Worth stating plainly rather than letting a working desk demo imply a working design: with those springs installed, the fin blows flat at speed. The flight part is a custom-wound left/right pair. Material selection got its own correction — I had 17-7 PH written down as the strong, marine-friendly escalation until I checked corrosion literature instead of vendor copy. Precipitation-hardened grades in aged spring temper are susceptible to chloride pitting and stress-corrosion cracking under sustained stress, which is precisely this service condition. Held at 302/304 stainless, with Elgiloy or MP35N as the real escalation path.

The single biggest lever on size turned out to be an inherited number: the hinge shaft diameter came from the bore of a catalog placeholder spring, not from any load calculation, and it had propagated into every downstream dimension. If the knuckles and pin carry the side load and the shaft is torque-only, it shrinks by nearly half — pulling coil diameter and the housing bulge down with it.

Prototypes: v1, a fracture, and the redesign it forced

The first printed prototype proved the kinematics — fold through the full sweep, no coil bind, clean spring-back.

v1 prototype — fold and spring-back

The second one broke — and it was the most useful result of the summer. The blade fractured at the barrel-to-root neck, cleanly along a layer plane. The cause was orientation, not material: layers had been stacked spanwise, so root bending pulled in tension across the layer welds, where PETG holds only 50–70% of its in-plane strength. Delamination, not material failure. Two fixes came out of it — reprint with the build direction perpendicular to the span, and add a 2–3 mm fillet at the neck, which the real metal part wants anyway regardless of how it’s made. The process fix solves the print; the geometry fix solves the part.

Standing caveat I kept attached to every printed part: no FDM component validates this structure. The best in-plane strength available is around 100 MPa against a real side-force case several times higher. Plastic prototypes here prove mechanism and geometry, never strength.

Structures: the load that sizes the blade

Drag sizes the spring; the lateral force sizes the blade — and it is what drives material selection. At a moderate sideslip the blade sees roughly 880 N, and because the section is thin, that load bends it about its weak axis, more than an order of magnitude worse than the strong one. Root stress lands where 6061 aluminum and 316 stainless both fail outright and 7075 has essentially no margin, leaving titanium or 17-4 PH as the credible options.

That is what drove the final configuration change: splitting one blade into two smaller ones. Total area is preserved, so directional authority is largely retained — about 85–90% of the single-fin yaw stiffness — while root bending per blade drops sharply, moving titanium from marginal to comfortable and adding redundancy if one blade is damaged. I did pick the less structurally optimal of two options here: a lower aspect ratio would have relieved more stress and opened up cheap aluminum, but it falls outside the aspect-ratio band the lead specified, so I stayed inside the requirement rather than optimizing my own metric.

The last iteration deleted every feature that disturbed flow — most importantly the crossbar at the top of the fin, replaced by bending the spring legs directly into the shaft. That change carries a risk I flagged rather than buried: a hole in the shaft is a stress concentration, and a tight leg bend is a classic torsion-spring fatigue initiation site, so the bend radius wants to stay at least twice the wire diameter.

Where it actually stands

Bench-proven kinematics and a design package — no vehicle integration, no on-water testing. That sentence is the honest boundary of this project and I won’t stretch it. What was demonstrated is the mechanism folding, deploying, and resetting through its full sweep without binding. What was not measured is torque, fold force, cycle life, or anything in water. The one quantitative bench result is the negative one: the fracture, and its root cause.

Handed off at internship end: the parametric mechanism CAD, the geometry generators, the validated 2D case plus the 3D case with a written post-mortem of what run 1 does and doesn’t prove, the twin-fin planform, and the spring specification with its procurement path. The named next steps are a boundary-layer-resolved 3D mesh to replace the estimated hinge moment with a measured one, a sideslip sweep on a mesh clean enough to trust, and confirming the cavitation margin — a first-pass minimum pressure coefficient exists and shows no cavitation at zero incidence, but the margin is thin enough to deserve the better mesh before anyone believes it.
