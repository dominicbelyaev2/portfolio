The Tele — One-Handed Infant Diapering

Role: 4-person team (Kaival Shah, Chenchen Wan, Eric Oh, Dominic Belyaev) — I worked on the sidebar mechanism, hinge integration and prototype fabrication · Tools: SOLIDWORKS, FDM 3D printing, woodshop fabrication · Timeline: Sep – Dec 2024 · Design Thinking & Communication, Segal Design Institute, Northwestern

Designed and built an assistive device that elevates an infant’s legs during a diaper change, so a parent with hemiplegia — who has the use of only one arm — can do it independently. Telescoping sidebars set leg height, elastic loops hold the ankles without pinning them, and every mechanism is operable with one hand. Built for a physical therapist at a rehabilitation hospital, from a first-principles observation of what actually makes diapering a two-handed job.

The problem

The brief came from Brianne Trainor, a physical therapist at Ascension Alexian Brothers Rehabilitation Hospital. Her patients with hemiplegia — paralysis down one side — have the use of one arm, and could not change their baby’s diaper without help. Existing products either ignore the one-armed case entirely or are too bulky to take anywhere.

Watching a conventional two-handed change is what made the constraint precise: one hand holds the infant’s legs up while the other cleans and places the diaper — and those two tasks overlap in time. That’s the whole reason one arm cannot do it. Naming that changed the project: we didn’t need to reinvent diapering, we needed to give the leg-holding job to a device and hand the working arm back to the user.

Requirements

  • Every interaction one-handed — unfold, adjust, load legs, refold. A step needing two hands invalidates the device.
  • Adjustable leg height, and enough range to cover a growing infant rather than one size.
  • Portable — folds down and travels, since changes don’t only happen at home.
  • Cleanable — it will get soiled; that’s a design input, not an afterthought.
  • Safe under an unpredictable load — infants move, and the restraint must hold without hurting them.

Ideation

We generated against three “how might we” framings — hold the legs up, keep the baby from moving, make the whole thing usable one-handed — before converging on the leg problem as the binding one.

The Decline won on function and then failed on mechanism. We built it with wooden slides to set leg height — and testing found two problems immediately: the slides had too much friction to move one-handed, and the notches used to hold height were fiddly to engage. User testing surfaced a third, more fundamental issue: the ramp blocked access to the baby’s back, which is exactly where you need to reach.

The Decline, built and loaded. The ramp holds the legs — and blocks the access you need underneath.

Operating it by hand during testing.

The pivot

Design review made the choice obvious. Reviewers pushed on the locking mechanism being insecure, on pinch points from too many moving parts, on materials that couldn’t be cleaned, and on not knowing the maximum infant weight the device could take.

So we stopped building a height mechanism and bought one. A rolling-suitcase handle already solves this exact problem — low-friction telescoping tubes, a button-actuated spring pin, discrete locking heights, engineered for one-handed use and millions of duty cycles. We bought a $55 suitcase and harvested its handle. The auto-locking hinges are folding table-leg brackets, chosen the same way: an off-the-shelf mechanism that already locks under load and releases on a button.

That decision is the one I’d defend hardest. Reusing a proven mechanism let a four-person team in a ten-week course spend its remaining time on the parts nobody sells — the leg loops, the geometry, the cleanability — instead of re-solving low-friction telescoping badly.

Final design

Height adjustment. Press to release, slide, let go — the pin drops into the next hole.

Auto-locking hinge. Clicks vertical under load, button-releases to fold.

Leg loops. Spaced apart, so the legs spread and the rear is reachable.

The loops are the piece that took the most iteration. String was tried first and cut into the legs. Fabric-covered elastic solved three things at once: it stretches enough to get a leg in one-handed, it distributes pressure over the ankle, and it leaves the infant enough movement not to fight the restraint. Spacing them apart across the handle spreads the legs and opens up the access that killed the ramp concept. The base is 3/8″ HDPE with a velcro-attached, machine-washable changing pad.

The HDPE base, with the washable pad going on over velcro corners.

Known limitations

Three came straight out of writing the instructions for use — documenting how something is operated is a good way to find what’s wrong with it.

  • Height is discrete, not continuous. The pin only locks where there’s a hole, so the user has to land on a set position. Inherited from the suitcase mechanism — the cost of reusing it.
  • Refolding needs both hinges released, one at a time. Each is a press-and-push, so folding down is the least one-handed part of the device and the first thing I’d redesign.
  • The loops need care on removal. The legs must be supported while the loops come off, or they drop. That’s a procedure step where it should be a design property.
  • Maximum infant weight was never established. Design review asked; we never answered it with a number. The device has no rated load.

What I’d change

  • Load-rate it. Take a 95th-percentile infant mass, apply it at the loops, and check the sidebars and hinge brackets in bending. Without that the safety case rests on the parts feeling sturdy.
  • Single-action fold. Link the two hinge releases so one press folds both, closing the last two-handed step.
  • Thinner base, lighter hinges. The HDPE is thicker than it needs to be and the locking table-leg brackets are heavy for the job.
  • Mould the handle. Printed was right for one-off; it’s the weakest part and the slowest to reproduce.
  • Test with the real users. Everything here was validated with an infant simulator. Until a parent with hemiplegia uses it unaided on a real infant, the central claim is unproven — and that’s the only test that settles it.

What I took away

Two things. The first is that the observation did more work than any of the engineering: we nearly designed a better diaper, and watching one real change is what redirected us to the leg problem. The second is that knowing when not to design something is a skill. The wooden slide was our own work and it didn’t function; a $55 suitcase handle did the job better than we were going to. Recognising that early bought us the weeks we needed for the parts that actually had to be invented.
