Single-Pivot, Side-Pull Bicycle Brake Caliper

Role: 4-person team — I was Testing & Validation Lead and FEA & Topology Lead, ran the topology optimization, and redesigned both arms for the final version · Tools: Siemens NX (CAD + Nastran FEA), Granta EduPack, SLS Nylon 12 · Timeline: Mar – Jun 2026 (ME 240)

Designed, analyzed, and physically validated a rear rim-brake caliper printed in SLS Nylon 12 against ISO 4210 braking requirements — the final test stopped the bike in 5.334 m against a 7.62 m ISO-adjusted limit, with FEA-predicted peak stress reduced from 48.66 to 40.81 MPa through a data-driven redesign.

Problem

Design a single-pivot, side-pull caliper for the rear wheel of a real test bicycle: transmit cable tension into pad clamping force, fit a constrained design space around the frame and rim, release cleanly, and stop the bike within the braking distances of ISO 4210-2 / 4210-4 — in a polymer (Nylon 12, ~50 MPa ultimate strength) far weaker than the aluminum it substitutes for.

Hand analysis before FEA

An energy balance (d = v²/2μg) connected braking distance to required rim friction, and a moment balance about the pivot (N = Fin L1/L2) set the arm lever ratio — more clamping force trades directly against pad travel. Section sizing at the arm root came from σ = P/A + Mc/I, which correctly predicted where FEA would later find the peak stress.

FEA (NX Nastran)

Each arm was meshed and solved separately: constrained at the pivot/mounting interfaces, loaded with the 89 N (20 lbf) cable force, stress read as element-nodal unaveraged von Mises. Singularities at constraints were excluded — reported maxima come from the physical load path. Pass criteria: peak stress under the 50 MPa static allowable, and total (left + right) deflection under 11 mm so the lever can't bottom out.

Test failure → redesign → pass

The initial arms came out of a topology optimization I ran on the design-space solid — that's where the organic cutouts in the initial right arm (below) come from. The print had acceptable FEA numbers but failed its road test: it was built around incorrect bike design-space dimensions, so the pads rubbed at rest and never contacted the rim squarely. I redesigned both arms around the verified geometry — resized so the pads met the rim flat and symmetrically, thickened root sections, reoriented the cable hole so the cable seated vertically, and added hardware clearance. Every change traced to a specific observed failure, and the redesign also improved the analysis: peak stress fell ~16% and the deflection safety factor rose from 1.23 to 1.45.

Validation on the bike

As testing lead I ran the physical program: rider accelerates to a stop line, brakes as hard as possible, and the stop distance is corrected to the ISO 4210-2 reference speed by dmax = dISO(vtest²/vISO²). The redesigned calipers stopped the bike in 5.334 m against the 7.62 m allowable, at 40 g per arm and 140 N actuation force.

Materials & production path

Beyond the printed prototype, a Granta EduPack screen (cost, salt-water corrosion, fracture toughness > 30 ksi√in, then strength- and stiffness-to-density) selected 7075-T6 aluminum for production, with volume-dependent processes: die casting + finish machining at high volume, forged 7075 + CNC for performance bikes, billet 6061 CNC for a frame shop. A fatigue check (Basquin S-N with Marin factors, Kf from notch sensitivity) showed the Nylon 12 part is a finite-life prototype — one reason the metal path matters.

What I'd change

Three metrics still failed at the final test: overall width (60.6 mm vs 55), released pad clearance (0.05 mm vs 0.3–2.0), and adjustment range (1 mm vs 2). The next iteration would narrow the arms ~6 mm, move the pad mounts outboard for real release clearance, and add a slotted pad boss — combining the corrected geometry with the more compact, material-efficient topology-optimized concept from my original design.
