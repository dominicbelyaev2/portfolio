Flight Telemetry Computer — Custom Logger for Rocket Flight 2

Role: solo — architecture, soldering, calibration, firmware, validation · Tools: Raspberry Pi Pico 2 (RP2350), CircuitPython, MPU6050 IMU, BMP388 barometer, perfboard, Python tooling · Timeline: Jul – Aug 2026 · flew twice 15 Aug 2026

A from-scratch flight computer that logs 100 Hz IMU + 50 Hz barometer data to onboard flash to recover a rocket’s top speed and apogee — designed, soldered, calibrated, and hardware-validated end-to-end: the flight firmware held full rate with zero sample skips, and the decoder’s pressure math cross-checks the sensor’s own compensation to 0.021 hPa. Then it flew twice on 15 Aug 2026: full-rate logging through boost on both flights, zero I²C errors through a ≥21 g ground impact, and the data diagnosed flight 1’s recovery failure between flights.

Architecture: each quantity gets the sensor that measures it best

QuantitySensorWhy
ApogeeBMP388 barometer @ 50 HzPressure altitude resolves ~0.13 m on the bench — GPS vertical error is 10–30× worse
Top speedMPU6050 accelerometer @ 100 HzIntegrated over the ~2 s boost; GPS updates too slowly and drops lock under acceleration
PositionGPS (parked)Recovery aid only — and the carbon nosecone blocks L1 from inside the bay, so it moved out of the flight build

The error budget drove the design: integrating acceleration for velocity works over a 2-second boost window precisely because bias contributes only b·t (~1 m/s) there, while a 3% scale error contributes ~4.5 m/s at 150 m/s — so calibration effort went into scale factor, not offset, and position was never attempted from the IMU at all (bias double-integrates to ~900 m of drift in a minute).

The build — and the three bugs that weren’t solder

The perfboard layout is generated by a Python script that emits the wiring diagram as SVG and validates itself — every Pico hole cross-checked against an independently derived pinout, plus net-clash and adjacency checks. That mattered because the map went through three corrections, each caught on the bench: the Pico’s two socket rows were swapped in the original orientation reasoning; both sensor breakouts were seated 180° reversed; and the 3.3 V rail was initially fed from 3V3_EN (the regulator’s enable pin) instead of 3V3_OUT — which produced a board that enumerated perfectly bare and died the instant any sensor drew current through the enable’s weak pull-up. Diagnosing that “works unloaded, collapses under load” signature down to a datasheet pin function is the debugging story I’d tell in any interview.

A quirk worth knowing: the RP2350’s internal pull-downs are broken (erratum E9), which turned my first software continuity test into 676 false faults. The tell was structure — every pin reading high is a silicon signature, not a solder one. The test was rewritten pull-ups-only and passed clean on all 26 GPIO.

Calibration: a tilt-immune 6-position solve

Accelerometer calibration ran on the finished rigid board in the flight range (±16 g), since sensitivity trim is per-range. Instead of the naive per-axis solve — which my off-vertical hand-holds would have corrupted by up to 0.9% — I solved bias and scale from the magnitude equation |(a − b)/s| = g on all six face means with Newton’s method: six equations, six unknowns, and tilt drops out of the math. Result: scale factor known to ~0.2% (vs the ±3% datasheet bound), taking the top-speed error contribution from ~4.5 m/s down to under 1 m/s.

Firmware built around the failure modes

  • Pad wait can’t exhaust flash: pre-launch samples circulate in a RAM chunk-ring — unlimited wait, zero flash wear — committed to flash only when launch is detected, so boost is recorded with no filesystem activity at all until a barrier sync at +6 s.
  • Ground impact can’t lose the data: fsync every 4 s after the barrier; the decoder treats a torn tail as data to drop, never to fabricate — a path that got exercised live when a Ctrl-C’d session truncated 157 records and the decoder dropped them cleanly.
  • Per-transaction I²C and file fault tolerance, hardware watchdog, LED status language for the pad.

Validation ran in layers: a synthetic-flight regression (generated binary log with known answers — decoder recovered apogee within 0.4 m and velocity exactly), then hardware: a dry-run rate test that held 100/50 Hz with zero skips and 4.4 ms worst-case loop time (settling the “do I need to port this to C++” question — no), then a 92-second end-to-end flight-mode log on the real board, decoded with a compensation cross-check that agreed with the sensor’s own math to −0.021 hPa. The review process also surfaced an upstream bug in the Adafruit MPU6050 driver — its filter_bandwidth property writes the wrong register bits — confirmed against the register map and worked around in my init code.

Mechanical integration: the printed housing and its load path

The first-rev printed sled (perfboard bosses, battery pocket, a scripted interference check reporting zero overlap across seven components) evolved into a printed housing that does double duty: it carries the boards and the recovery loads. The load path runs shock cord → quick link → #8-32 eye bolt → the housing’s base flange → heat-set inserts and M2 lid screws → lid → threaded rod → the nosecone’s metal tip — printed parts carrying recovery load at a uniform SF ≈ 2 against the ~300 N deployment spike, consciously accepted because every link sits at the same margin. The detail I’d defend in a review: the nylock that retains the eye bolt seats in a recess cut into a flange grown from 4 to 8 mm rather than thinned — leaving a 3 mm web at shear SF ≈ 5 — because you can’t counterbore 5 mm into a 4 mm wall. One bench finding became a design input: the barometer’s reading scatters ~6× worse in moving air than on the still bench, so the bay sees baffled static pressure rather than a straight shot of airflow.

Flown — and what two flights proved

Before flight day, the launch-detect path got its hardware test the cheap way: a windmill-swing on battery power (~3 g sustained) fired LAUNCH→boost-hold→LANDED live on the board. Then both flights on 15 Aug 2026 exercised everything at once: 100/50 Hz held through boost on both, the in-flight pressure cross-checks passed (+0.274 and +0.022 hPa), the ±16 g range decision held with margin (6 g boost, 2.7×), and flight 2’s 15.5 g deployment shock was captured 0.4 g under the ceiling. Flight 1 also delivered the failure the firmware was designed around — and one it wasn’t: the ballistic landing didn’t hurt the data, but cutting power eight minutes later mid-commit corrupted the filesystem, and the log survived only by raw-imaging and carving the flash.

The v2 list is now flight-derived, not speculative: finalize the log file once LANDED is detected (the corruption window was the post-landing commits, not the flight); flush the pre-launch ring buffer incrementally (the current single flush blinds both sensors for ~3 s at t+6.00 — flight 2’s deployment cleared that window by 0.47 s); and the KiCad carrier PCB in place of perfboard. This computer flew both flights of the campaign in the carbon nosecone it was built for.
