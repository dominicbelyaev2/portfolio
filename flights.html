Two Flights, One Afternoon

Role: solo — integration, launch ops, data recovery & analysis · Vehicle: 2.3-in airframe, AeroTech F67W-4, molded carbon nosecone, custom flight computer · Flown: 15 Aug 2026

Left: flight 1 — the chute never opened. Right: flight 2, an hour later — clean deploy and recovery.

Two flights of the same rocket in one afternoon: flight 1’s parachute jammed and the airframe came down ballistic at 17 m/s — flight 2, after a repack, deployed cleanly and landed at 5.1 m/s. The flight computer logged both end-to-end, the data diagnosed the failure between flights, and boost repeated to about 2% across the pair. That repeatability is the real result: airframe, avionics, and analysis chain all did their jobs twice.

The descent-rate pair — 17.2 vs 5.1 m/s, measured by the same instrument on the same day — is the direct proof the repack fixed deployment. And flight 2’s lower apogee on an identical boost isn’t a performance gap: the reconstruction below shows it weathercocked into wind and spent the same energy arcing downrange.

Assembly & launch ops

Everything upstream converges here: the carbon shell seats over the printed coupler, the flight computer rides in its printed housing on the eye-bolt recovery load path, and the whole nose section comes in around 250 g. Flown at a club launch — flight card filed, RSO inspection, club pad and rail.

Flight 1: the charge fired — the chute never left the tube

The ejection charge fired 4.0 s after burnout, 0.1 s after barometric apogee — a +31 hPa pressure spike in the bay makes the timing unambiguous. So the failure was mechanical, not timing: the chute stayed packed in the tube, and the rocket came down tumbling at 17 m/s. The impact number is a floor, not a measurement — one accelerometer axis hit its ±16 g ceiling, so ≥21.2 g is as much as the data can honestly claim. The airframe survived reflyable, which is its own small validation of the build.

The data almost died with the landing

Eight minutes after landing I cut power while the logger was still committing; the FAT corrupted, and on the next boot the board reformatted itself — the drive mounted factory-fresh. A reformat rewrites allocation tables, not data blocks, so I raw-imaged the 3 MB volume read-only before any writes and carved the log back out of the image: 47,000 IMU and 24,000 barometer records recovered, with the decoder’s pressure cross-check passing at +0.27 hPa. The rule that saved the flight: image first, experiment on the copy.

Flight 2: repack, refly, full success

Same rocket, same motor, about an hour later. Clean deploy, 5.1 m/s under canopy, and a boost profile that matched flight 1 to ~2% — the flight-to-flight repeatability that makes the rest of the numbers trustworthy. The opening shock logged 15.5 g, captured unclipped — but only because the charge fired 1.1 s past apogee at ~10 m/s of descent. Opening shock scales roughly with speed squared, so that 15.5 g is the load at a 10 m/s deploy, not a constant of the rocket.

Reconstructing the flights in 3D

Both trajectories were rebuilt from the raw logs: gyro bias re-estimated from 60 s of still pad data, attitude integrated at 100 Hz, body acceleration rotated to the world frame and double-integrated, with the barometer held out as an independent validation channel. The IMU-only altitude — never shown the barometer — agreed with it to +1.3 m (flight 1) and +3.3 m (flight 2) at apogee, which is the license to read the attitude results quantitatively. They settle the apogee question: flight 2’s nose was 27.6° off vertical at burnout vs flight 1’s 4.6° — weathercocking into wind, arcing ~30 m downrange on the same energy. The solutions are only quoted from launch to ejection/deploy (the tumble and the near-clip opening shock corrupt the attitude after that), and with no magnetometer aboard, headings are relative, not compass bearings.

What the data changed for v2

  • The logger goes blind for ~3 s at t+6.00 while the pre-launch ring buffer flushes to flash — flight 2’s deployment event cleared that window by 0.47 s. The v2 firmware flushes incrementally so no flight event can land in a gap.
  • Finalize the log file after landing is detected. The filesystem corruption window existed because the logger kept committing for eight minutes after touchdown; closing the file at LANDED plus a “safe to power off” LED removes it.
  • Deployment loads get quoted with deploy speed attached — motor delay scatter alone can push the opening shock past the ±16 g accelerometer range.

This campaign is the capstone of three builds: the L1 certification that started the program, the molded carbon nosecone, and the flight computer that measured all of it.
