Carbon Fiber Nosecone — Mandrel Failure to Molded Part

Role: solo — geometry, tooling design, mold fabrication, layup · Tools: Fusion 360, QIDI Q2 FDM, West System 105/206, vacuum bagging · Timeline: Jul – Aug 2026 · flown twice 15 Aug 2026

A Von Kármán carbon fiber nosecone for my rocket’s instrumented second flight — laid up in a split female mold I designed in code and 3D-printed, closed wet around an internal pressure bladder, and demolded as a seamless shell the next morning. The tooling exists because Plan A, a dissolvable printed mandrel, failed at demold and taught me why.

Problem

Replace the rocket’s stock plastic nosecone with a self-made composite one, sized to the real airframe: the profile is an equation-driven Von Kármán (Haack C = 0) curve, 12.25 in long, matched to the 2.289 in body tube and a screw-retained metal tip. The interesting constraint isn’t flight loads — laminate stress is ~1–2 MPa against a >200 MPa laminate, so strength never sizes the wall. The whole project is a manufacturing problem: hitting the outer mold line, the tip-plug bore, and the body-tube fit with backyard tooling.

Plan A: dissolvable mandrel — and why it failed

The first build wrapped five plies of fiberglass around a 3D-printed mandrel: a ribbed PLA core inside a 0.05 in PVA (water-soluble) shell, filled with sand so a 6–9 inHg vacuum bag couldn’t crush it. The layup itself worked — the bag consolidated cleanly. Demolding didn’t. Dissolution physics beat me: water could only attack the PVA from the exposed base edge, advancing about an inch per half hour, and an overnight full-submersion soak still didn’t release the core because water never reached the glass–PVA interface. The core came out destructively — boiling-water floods, snapping ribs through the sand pockets, peeling the skin in strips. Lesson: on a closed tapered part, plan for mechanical extraction; dissolution only clears the residual film afterward.

Plan B: a split female mold, built in Fusion 360

The rebuild inverted the tooling: a two-half female mold whose cavity is the aero outer surface, so the OD comes off the mold and all ply-thickness scatter lands harmlessly on the ID. I designed the mold in Fusion 360 as a fully parametric model — the equation-driven cavity curve, bolted flanges (12× 1/4-20), witness grooves at the trim stations, pry notches — and rebuilt it freely as the design evolved (the tip sections got 28% shallower in one dimension change, saving three hours of printing each). Each half prints in two sections on a 270 mm bed, joined with dowels and adhesive, then the cavities got two skim coats of thin epoxy and a wet-sand pass to become mold surfaces.

One-shot closed-mold layup

Instead of laying up each half separately and bonding two cured shells, I wet-laid both halves open and closed the mold while everything was still wet, so fiber runs continuously across the seams and there is no secondary bond line. One half’s plies were cut exactly to the cavity edge; the other half’s carried 0.5 in flaps that lap across the parting line onto the first half’s inner surface — a detail rehearsed dry before any resin was mixed, because the flaps near the tip get placed blind. Consolidation came from an internal film-wrapped bladder at 2–3 psi pressing the laminate into the cavity, with the mold bolted against roughly 60 lb of opening force. Four plies of 193 gsm twill (Levanta scrap stock — measured, not assumed), targeting the same ~0.035 in wall the glass design used. Demolded the next morning: mold-surface finish outside, sandable bladder-side ridges inside, both accepted trades I’d made on paper first.

Decisions that made it work

  • Envelope-bag logic: a printed mold is not a vacuum barrier — layer lines, infill, and twelve through-bolt holes all leak. Every consolidation scheme was designed so the film, not the print, holds pressure.
  • Cured-ply arithmetic: wall = plies × cured thickness, and cured thickness depends on fiber volume fraction, which depends on consolidation pressure. At the bladder’s 2–3 psi the laminate runs resin-rich (~0.009 in/ply), which is why the ply count stayed at four — a fifth would overshoot the metal tip’s bore.
  • Trim as the recovery lever: the tip station is cut after measuring the real cured wall — the cone’s slope converts a 0.03 in station shift into ~0.004 in of radius, absorbing the process scatter the low-pressure cure creates.

The mass delta, on a scale

The payoff of the pivot shows up on a kitchen scale: the carbon shell weighs 102.4 g against 194.0 g for the try-1 fiberglass build — 47% lighter for the same outer mold line, weighed on the same scale in the same session. The honest breakdown of that delta: one fewer ply (the female mold’s smooth surface deleted the sanding-margin ply), carbon’s lower areal weight for the same stiffness class, far less resin retained by the bladder-consolidated laminate than the 6–9 inHg mandrel bag — and the fiberglass cone carries its paint, so the comparison is build-vs-build, not a pure materials number.

Flown — twice in one afternoon

The shell flew both flights of the instrumented campaign on 15 Aug 2026, with the flight computer in the nosecone bay logging 100 Hz IMU and 50 Hz baro: boost repeatable to ~2% (6.0 / 6.1 g), measured apogees of 84.6 and 76.9 m, and — unplanned but informative — a structural proof: flight 1’s parachute never deployed and the shell took a 17 m/s ballistic landing reflyable, flying again an hour later. The OpenRocket-predicted apogee (~120 m) came in well above the measured values; closing that predicted-vs-actual loop against the logged drag and the reconstructed weathercocking is the next analysis. One real trade came out of going carbon: the conductive shell blocks GPS from inside the cone, so the GPS moved out of the flight build and recovery tracking relocates to the fiberglass body tube.

What I’d change

The cured-per-ply thickness is still an estimate bracketed by fiber-volume-fraction assumptions — the test coupon I keep planning would replace that assumption with a measurement. The bladder’s 2–3 psi is far below a proper vacuum consolidation, so the laminate carries more resin than it needs; a compaction scheme that safely reaches higher pressure (or an infusion setup like the ones I used at my internship) is the next process step. And the mandrel failure was avoidable on paper: I never sanity-checked the dissolution front rate against the geometry before committing a 4-hour print and a layup to it.
